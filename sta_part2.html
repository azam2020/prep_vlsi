Chapter 4: Static Timing Analysis — Part 2 (Q51 to Q100)

PVT Corners, Temperature Inversion, OCV/AOCV, CPPR, and Advanced Numericals

6. PVT Variations & Operating Corners

Q51 What does PVT stand for in VLSI?

Answer: Process, Voltage, and Temperature. These are the three physical factors that dramatically affect the delay (speed) of logic gates and wires on a silicon die.

Q52 Explain Process (P) variation.

Answer: During fabrication, absolute perfection is impossible. Wafer-to-wafer or lot-to-lot variations cause changes in transistor channel lengths ($L$), oxide thickness ($T_{ox}$), and doping concentrations. This causes identical transistors to switch slightly faster or slower than intended.

Q53 What are the standard Silicon Process Corners?

Answer:
• TT (Typical-Typical): Nominal NMOS and nominal PMOS.
• SS (Slow-Slow): Slow NMOS, Slow PMOS (Highest delay, lowest current).
• FF (Fast-Fast): Fast NMOS, Fast PMOS (Lowest delay, highest leakage).
• FS / SF: Fast-NMOS/Slow-PMOS and Slow-NMOS/Fast-PMOS (Cross corners).

Q54 How does Voltage (V) affect delay?

Answer: A lower supply voltage ($V_{DD}$) decreases the overdrive voltage ($V_{GS} - V_{TH}$), which severely reduces the transistor's drive current ($I_D$). Therefore, lower voltage = slower delay. Higher voltage = faster delay.

Q55 In traditional (older) technology nodes, how does Temperature (T) affect delay?

Answer: As temperature increases, the thermal vibrations of the silicon lattice increase. This causes carrier scattering, which reduces electron/hole mobility ($\mu$). Lower mobility means lower drive current. Therefore, higher temperature = slower delay.

Q56 Define the worst-case corner for Setup Time checks.

Answer: Setup requires data to arrive as fast as possible. The worst-case is when the data path is extremely slow.
Corner: Slow Process (SS), Low Voltage, High Temperature (usually $125^\circ C$). This is often called the Worst Case (WC) or Max corner.

Q57 Define the worst-case corner for Hold Time checks.

Answer: Hold requires data to arrive slowly so it doesn't corrupt the current capture. The worst-case is when the data path is terrifyingly fast.
Corner: Fast Process (FF), High Voltage, Low Temperature (usually $-40^\circ C$). This is called the Best Case (BC) or Min corner.

Q58 What is an RC Interconnect Corner?

Answer: Just like transistors, the metal wires (interconnects) have variations in thickness and width due to etching/CMP processes, affecting Resistance (R) and Capacitance (C).
• Cmax / Cworst: Max capacitance, lowest resistance. (Used for Max delay).
• Cmin / Cbest: Min capacitance, highest resistance. (Used for Min delay).
• RCworst: Max resistance, max capacitance.

Q59 What is MMMC (Multi-Mode Multi-Corner) analysis?

Answer: Modern SoCs operate in different modes (e.g., Sleep mode, High-Performance mode, Test mode) and must survive across all corners (SS, FF, TT). MMMC is the automated STA process of analyzing all combinations of Modes and Corners simultaneously to ensure zero timing violations anywhere.

Q60 When do FS (Fast-Slow) or SF (Slow-Fast) process corners cause the most damage?

Answer: These cross corners severely affect Noise Margins and Duty Cycle. For example, in an SF corner (Slow NMOS, Fast PMOS), the rise time is extremely fast, but the fall time is sluggish. This skews the Switching Threshold ($V_M$) high, making the inverter vulnerable to ground bounce (logic 0 noise).

7. Temperature Inversion (Sub-micron Phenomena)

Q61 What is Temperature Inversion?

Answer: In advanced technology nodes (e.g., sub-65nm) operating at very low supply voltages, the traditional rule of "High Temp = Slower" flips. Instead, Low Temperature results in Slower delay, and High Temperature results in Faster delay. This reversal is called Temperature Inversion.

Q62 What is the physical physics mechanism driving Temperature Inversion?

Answer: Transistor current depends on two factors that react oppositely to temperature:
1. Mobility ($\mu$): Decreases at high temp (makes gate slower).
2. Threshold Voltage ($V_{TH}$): Decreases at high temp (makes gate faster).
At ultra-low $V_{DD}$ (e.g., 0.7V), the overdrive voltage ($V_{DD} - V_{TH}$) is extremely tiny. At low temperatures, $V_{TH}$ increases so much that the gate barely turns on. This loss of overdrive dominates over the mobility gain, crippling the current and making the gate severely slow at cold temperatures.

Q63 How does Temperature Inversion change Setup Time STA checks?

Answer: You can no longer assume $125^\circ C$ is the worst-case corner for Setup. You must perform Setup checks at BOTH $125^\circ C$ (traditional max) AND $-40^\circ C$ (inversion max) to guarantee the chip won't fail at extreme cold.

Q64 What is the "Inversion Temperature"?

Answer: The exact temperature threshold where the mobility dominance (traditional curve) and $V_{TH}$ dominance (inversion curve) intersect. Below this temperature, the delay curve inverts and goes up.

Q65 Tricky Does Temperature Inversion affect metal interconnect (wire) delay?

Answer: NO. Wires are pure conductors. As temperature drops, metal resistance drops linearly. Wires are ALWAYS faster at low temperatures. Temperature Inversion is strictly a transistor (silicon) phenomenon.

Trap Used: If a timing path is heavily dominated by wire RC delay rather than gate delay, the path might still follow traditional scaling (slower at high temp). This is why multi-corner analysis is mandatory.

8. On-Chip Variation (OCV, AOCV, CPPR)

Q66 What is On-Chip Variation (OCV)?

Answer: Even on the exact same silicon die, operating at the exact same global PVT corner, two identical transistors placed millimeters apart will have slightly different delays. This is due to local micro-variations in etching, local hot-spots (temperature gradients), and IR drop. OCV models this local unpredictability.

Q67 How is Flat OCV applied to a Setup Time check?

Answer: OCV intentionally penalizes the math to make the check harder to pass (pessimism).
• We assume the Data Path and Launch Clock are arbitrarily SLOW (apply a `late` derate factor, e.g., $\times 1.1$).
• We assume the Capture Clock path is arbitrarily FAST (apply an `early` derate factor, e.g., $\times 0.9$).

Q68 How is Flat OCV applied to a Hold Time check?

Answer:
• We assume the Data Path and Launch Clock are arbitrarily FAST (`early` derate, e.g., $\times 0.9$).
• We assume the Capture Clock path is arbitrarily SLOW (`late` derate, e.g., $\times 1.1$).

Q69 What is the fatal flaw with "Flat OCV" in modern technology nodes?

Answer: Flat OCV applies a blanket multiplier (like 10% penalty) to every single gate regardless of the path length. In reality, random variations statistically cancel each other out over long paths (Law of Large Numbers). Flat OCV is too pessimistic, forcing tools to over-optimize and waste massive area/power fixing phantom violations.

Q70 What is AOCV (Advanced OCV)?

Answer: AOCV uses 2D look-up tables based on Logic Depth and Physical Distance. A very deep logic path (e.g., 20 gates) will receive a much smaller derating penalty (e.g., 2%) than a shallow path (e.g., 2 gates, 12% penalty) because random variations average out over depth.

Q71 What is POCV (Parametric OCV) or SOCV (Statistical OCV)?

Answer: At 7nm and below, even AOCV is too inaccurate. POCV stops using absolute min/max multipliers. Instead, it models the delay of every single cell as a statistical Gaussian distribution ($\mu, \sigma$). The delay of a path is calculated statistically as $D = \mu + c \cdot \sigma$ (where $c$ is typically 3 for $3\sigma$ confidence).

Q72 What is LVF (Liberty Variance Format)?

Answer: It is an extension to the standard Liberty (`.lib`) timing file format. Standard `.lib` contains fixed delay values ($T_{cq}, T_{pd}$). LVF files contain the standard deviation ($\sigma$) values required by STA tools to calculate POCV statistical timing.

Q73 What is CPPR (Clock Path Pessimism Removal) or CRPR (Clock Reconvergence Pessimism Removal)?

Answer: When applying OCV, we make the launch clock path SLOW and the capture clock path FAST. However, the launch and capture clocks share the same physical buffers at the root of the clock tree before diverging. A single physical buffer cannot be fast and slow at the exact same time. CPPR calculates the artificial penalty applied to this common path and mathematically adds it back to the slack to remove the false pessimism.

Clock Path Pessimism Removal (CPPR)

For Setup OCV, Cb is calculated as SLOW for Launch and FAST for Capture. CPPR fixes this impossible paradox.

Q74 Write the equation to calculate the CPPR adjustment value.
$CPPR_{setup} = Common\_Delay_{Late} - Common\_Delay_{Early}$

This positive value is added directly to the Setup Slack to improve the margin.

Q75 Tricky Does CPPR apply to the Data Path?

Answer: NO. The data path does not diverge and reconverge with itself. CPPR only applies to the Clock Tree, specifically the common segment from the clock root (PLL) down to the divergence node where it splits to the launch and capture flip-flops.

9. Advanced Interview Numericals

Q76 Numerical Calculate Setup Slack with OCV.
Given: $T_{clk} = 10ns$, $T_{su} = 1ns$, $T_{cq(max)} = 2ns$, $T_{comb(max)} = 5ns$.
Launch clock path delay = $3ns$. Capture clock path delay = $3ns$.
OCV Derate Factors: Late = $1.2$, Early = $0.9$. (Ignore CPPR for now).

Answer:
1. Arrival Time (Late):
Launch Clock (Late) = $3ns \times 1.2 = 3.6ns$
Data Path (Late) = $(T_{cq} + T_{comb}) \times 1.2 = (2 + 5) \times 1.2 = 8.4ns$
Total Arrival Time = $3.6 + 8.4 = 12.0ns$

2. Required Time (Early):
Capture Clock (Early) = $(T_{clk} + Path\_Delay) \times 0.9 = (10 + 3) \times 0.9 = 11.7ns$
Required Time = Capture Clock - $T_{su} = 11.7 - 1.0 = 10.7ns$

3. Setup Slack: Required - Arrival = $10.7 - 12.0 = \mathbf{-1.3ns}$ (Violation!)

Note: Without OCV, the slack was $(10 + 3 - 1) - (3 + 2 + 5) = 12 - 10 = +2ns$. OCV flipped a passing path into a failing path!
Q77 Numerical Calculate CPPR for the previous question. Assume the common clock path delay (before divergence) is $2ns$ (nominal).

Answer:
Common path nominal = $2ns$.
Common path Late evaluated = $2ns \times 1.2 = 2.4ns$
Common path Early evaluated = $2ns \times 0.9 = 1.8ns$
CPPR Credit = $2.4ns - 1.8ns = \mathbf{0.6ns}$.
New Setup Slack = $-1.3ns + 0.6ns = \mathbf{-0.7ns}$. (Still failing, but less pessimistic).

Q78 Numerical Calculate Hold Slack with OCV.
Given: $T_h = 0.5ns$, $T_{cq(min)} = 1.5ns$, $T_{comb(min)} = 1.0ns$.
Launch clock delay = $2ns$, Capture clock delay = $2ns$.
OCV Derates: Late = $1.2$, Early = $0.9$.

Answer: For Hold, Data is FAST (Early), Capture Clock is SLOW (Late).
1. Arrival Time (Early):
Launch Clock = $2ns \times 0.9 = 1.8ns$
Data Path = $(1.5 + 1.0) \times 0.9 = 2.25ns$
Total Arrival Time = $1.8 + 2.25 = \mathbf{4.05ns}$

2. Required Time (Late):
Capture Clock = $2ns \times 1.2 = 2.4ns$
Required Time = Capture Clock + $T_h = 2.4 + 0.5 = \mathbf{2.9ns}$

3. Hold Slack: Arrival - Required = $4.05 - 2.9 = \mathbf{+1.15ns}$ (Pass).

Q79 How do you fix the $-0.7ns$ Setup violation from Q77 without changing the clock frequency?

Answer: Use Useful Skew. If we intentionally add $0.7ns$ of buffer delay to the non-common segment of the Capture Clock path, the Required Time shifts out by $0.7ns$ (adjusting for OCV derate), closing the negative slack to exactly $0ns$.

Q80 Numerical A FF has $T_{cq} = 2ns, T_{su} = 1ns, T_{comb} = 5ns$. There is $0.5ns$ of Clock Jitter. Find $F_{max}$.

Answer:
$T_{clk} \ge T_{cq} + T_{comb} + T_{su} + T_{jitter}$
$T_{clk} \ge 2 + 5 + 1 + 0.5 = 8.5ns$
$F_{max} = 1 / 8.5ns \approx \mathbf{117.6 \text{ MHz}}$

Q81 What is Time Borrowing in Latch-based designs?

Answer: Unlike an edge-triggered FF, a level-sensitive latch is transparent for an entire clock phase (e.g., $CLK=1$). If the combinational logic driving the latch is slightly too slow and arrives after the rising edge, it can just "borrow" time from the high phase. The signal passes through immediately, eating into the time budget of the next logic stage.

Q82 Numerical Latch 1 and Latch 2 are transparent high. Duty cycle is 50%. $T_{clk} = 10ns$. Logic between them takes $6ns$. How much time is borrowed?

Answer: The time available before the rising edge of L2 is $T_{clk}/2 = 5ns$.
Since the logic takes $6ns$, the data arrives $1ns$ after L2 opens.
Because L2 is transparent high (from $5ns$ to $10ns$), the data safely passes through.
Time Borrowed = $1ns$. The logic stage after L2 now only has $4ns$ left to complete its work instead of $5ns$.

Q83 Why do we use Flip-Flops in standard SoCs if Latches allow Time Borrowing?

Answer:
1. Hold Time Nightmares: If logic between latches is too fast, data shoots straight through both latches while they are transparent, causing catastrophic race conditions.
2. Tool Complexity: Standard STA tools easily analyze rigid edge-to-edge paths. Analyzing fluid time-borrowing across millions of latches is computationally immense.

Q84 What is a "Half-Cycle Path", and when does it occur?

Answer: It occurs when a Launch FF is triggered on the Rising Edge, and the Capture FF is triggered on the Falling Edge of the same clock. The data only has $T_{clk}/2$ to travel. It severely tightens the Setup check, but naturally provides immense Hold time margin.

Q85 How do you constraint an asynchronous clock crossing in your SDC (Synopsys Design Constraints) file?

Answer: You must declare them as asynchronous or false paths so the STA tool doesn't try to mathematically meet setup/hold between them. 

set_clock_groups -asynchronous -group {clk_A} -group {clk_B}

Q86 What is the difference between `set_false_path` and `set_clock_groups`?

Answer:
• set_false_path: Disables timing checks on specific topological paths (e.g., from a specific register to an output).
• set_clock_groups: Globally tells the tool that two entire clock domains have no phase relationship, disabling all checks between every register in domain A and domain B simultaneously.

Q87 Define "Virtual Clock" in STA.

Answer: A clock defined in the SDC file that does not exist as a physical pin on the chip. It is used as a reference to constrain the timing of input/output ports relative to the external board-level components.

Q88 What does `set_input_delay` actually constrain?

Answer: It tells the STA tool how much time was already consumed by external logic on the PCB before the signal reached the chip's input port.
If $T_{clk} = 10ns$ and `set_input_delay = 4ns`, the internal chip logic only has $6ns$ left to capture the data.

Q89 What does `set_output_delay` constrain?

Answer: It tells the STA tool how much setup time the external PCB logic requires after the signal leaves the chip.
If $T_{clk} = 10ns$ and `set_output_delay = 3ns`, the internal chip logic must push the data to the output pad within $7ns$.

Q90 What is an unconstrained path, and why is it dangerous?

Answer: A path that has no clock or external delay definitions applied to it. The synthesis tool optimizes unconstrained paths for zero area (using the slowest, smallest gates possible). If that path was actually functionally critical, the chip will fail. The rule is: 100% of I/O ports must be constrained.

Q91 What is `set_max_delay` used for?

Answer: It explicitly forces the tool to limit the propagation delay of a path to a specific absolute time, regardless of the clock period. Often used for combinational In-to-Out paths or asynchronous crossings.

Q92 Tricky If you have a Hold violation of $-0.2ns$, and you insert a delay buffer of $0.3ns$ to fix it, what happens to your Setup Slack?

Answer: Your Setup Slack degrades by exactly $0.3ns$. Adding delay to the data path helps Hold but directly hurts Setup. You must ensure you have at least $+0.3ns$ of positive Setup margin before inserting that buffer!

Q93 Why do we perform "Crosstalk Timing Analysis" (Delta Delay)?

Answer: Wires routed parallel to each other have parasitic coupling capacitance. If an "Aggressor" wire transitions from 0-to-1 at the exact same time a "Victim" wire transitions from 1-to-0, the crosstalk fights the transition, slowing the victim down (Setup penalty). If they transition in the same direction, the victim speeds up (Hold penalty).

Q94 What is Signal Integrity (SI) - Glitch Analysis?

Answer: If the Victim wire is completely static (holding logic 0) and a massive Aggressor wire switches, it can inject a voltage spike (glitch) onto the Victim. If that glitch exceeds $V_{IH}$ of the receiving gate, it causes a false logical transition.

Q95 How do you fix a Crosstalk/SI violation in Physical Design?

Answer:
1. Spacing: Physically move the wires further apart (Capacitance $\propto 1/distance$).
2. Shielding: Route a grounded $V_{SS}$ wire between the Aggressor and Victim.
3. Upsizing: Increase the drive strength of the Victim gate so it holds its state more rigidly against the noise.

Q96 What is the "Derating Factor" applied during Crosstalk Delay analysis?

Answer: To account for the worst-case timing window alignment (where Aggressor and Victim switch simultaneously), tools apply an SI derating multiplier to the coupling capacitance, effectively assuming $C_{eff} = 2 \times C_{coupling}$ for opposing transitions.

Q97 What is a "Timing Exception"?

Answer: Any SDC constraint that overrides the default single-cycle STA behavior. Examples include False Paths, Multi-Cycle Paths (MCP), Min/Max Delays, and Clock Groups.

Q98 What happens if you define a path as BOTH a Multi-Cycle Path and a False Path?

Answer: False Path wins. SDC constraints have a strict priority hierarchy. False Path is the highest priority exception. The tool will completely ignore the MCP constraint and cease analyzing the path.

Q99 In PrimeTime, what does the command `report_timing` output?

Answer: It generates a detailed timing report showing the Worst Negative Slack (WNS). It lists the startpoint, endpoint, path group, required time, arrival time, and a node-by-node breakdown of incremental delays (cell delay + net delay) along the critical path.

Q100 The Final Test: What are WNS and TNS?

Answer:
• WNS (Worst Negative Slack): The slack of the single worst failing path in the entire chip. Determines if the chip meets the target frequency.
• TNS (Total Negative Slack): The mathematical sum of the negative slack from all failing endpoints. It tells you how fundamentally broken the design is. (e.g., A WNS of -10ps is bad, but a TNS of -50000ps means thousands of paths are failing, requiring a massive re-design).
