Chapter 5: Physical Design — Part 2 (Q51 to Q100)

Clock Tree Synthesis (CTS), Routing, DRC/LVS Verification, and ECOs

5. Clock Tree Synthesis (CTS)

Q51 What is Clock Tree Synthesis (CTS)?

Answer: CTS is the process of building a balanced buffer tree to distribute the clock signal from a single source (like a PLL) to thousands or millions of flip-flops (sinks) across the chip. The primary goals are to minimize Clock Skew and minimize Insertion Delay.

Q52 Define Insertion Delay (Clock Latency).

Answer: The total time it takes for the clock signal to travel from its physical source (root) to the clock pin of the target flip-flop (sink).

  • Source Latency: Delay from the true clock origin (e.g., off-chip oscillator) to the root pin of the chip.
  • Network Latency: Delay from the root pin of the chip through the internal clock tree to the flip-flop.
Q53 Why is minimizing Insertion Delay critical for OCV (On-Chip Variation)?

Answer: A clock tree with a massive insertion delay (e.g., 2ns) requires many buffers. OCV derating applies a percentage penalty to the entire path (e.g., 10%). $10\%$ of 2ns is 200ps of artificial skew penalty. If the insertion delay is small (e.g., 0.5ns), the OCV penalty is only 50ps, making timing closure much easier.

Q54 How is a Clock Buffer different from a standard logic Buffer?

Answer: Standard buffers are optimized for area and may have unequal rise and fall times. Clock Buffers are rigorously designed to have perfectly equal rise and fall times (symmetrical PMOS/NMOS drive strengths) to maintain an exact 50% duty cycle across the entire clock tree.

Q55 Why do physical designers prefer using Inverters instead of Buffers in the clock tree?

Answer: A buffer is physically just two inverters in series. Using single inverters reduces the total number of transistors in the tree (saving Area and Dynamic Power) and reduces the total insertion delay. Furthermore, alternating the signal phase helps balance duty-cycle distortions caused by unequal rise/fall times.

Q56 What are Non-Default Rules (NDR) in CTS?

Answer: Standard metal wires are routed using minimum width and minimum spacing rules to save area. Clock nets toggle constantly and are highly susceptible to EM and RC delay. CTS uses NDR—typically Double Width and Double Spacing (2W2S)—to lower resistance, reduce RC delay, and drastically cut crosstalk from neighboring nets.

Q57 Tricky What is Clock Net Shielding, and what does it physically look like?

Answer: Shielding protects the highly sensitive clock net from Crosstalk/SI glitches. The router physically places continuous GND ($V_{SS}$) wires immediately adjacent to both the left and right sides of the clock wire along its entire route. This guarantees the clock net only capacitively couples to a stable ground, immune to aggressor toggling.

Q58 What is an H-Tree Clock distribution?

Answer: A symmetric, fractal-like geometric routing structure shaped like the letter "H". The clock enters the center, splits left/right, then up/down. Because the physical wire lengths from the root to every endpoint are mathematically identical, an H-tree provides theoretically zero skew (ignoring OCV).

Q59 What is a Clock Mesh, and why is it used in high-performance CPUs?

Answer: Instead of a tree branching to endpoints, the clock is driven onto a massive, continuous grid (mesh) of thick metal covering the entire core. Sinks tap directly into the mesh above them. This consumes immense routing area and power, but provides the absolute lowest possible skew and highest resilience to OCV.

Q60 Why does the number of Hold Time violations explode immediately after CTS?

Answer: During Placement, clocks are considered "Ideal" (zero delay, zero skew). After CTS, the actual physical clock tree is built, introducing real Clock Skew. Since Hold Time is incredibly sensitive to skew (Capture clock arriving early closes the hold window too fast), thousands of paths that passed ideally will fail realistically.

Q61 How does the tool fix Post-CTS Hold violations?

Answer: It performs Hold Fixing by physically inserting delay buffers directly into the fast data paths. This slows the data down so it safely arrives after the hold window has closed.

Q62 Where is the optimal placement for an Integrated Clock Gating (ICG) cell?

Answer: As close to the root of the clock tree as possible. If placed near the root, disabling the ICG shuts off the clock to the entire downstream buffer tree and all its sinks, maximizing dynamic power savings.

Q63 Whiteboard How do you balance a clock tree when Flip-Flop A is 10mm away from the root, and Flip-Flop B is 1mm away?

Answer: The tool uses "Snaking" (intentional wire detouring) and adds dummy delay buffers to the path of Flip-Flop B. By artificially slowing down the fast path, the arrival times are equalized, achieving zero skew.

Q64 Are clock nets routed before or after standard data nets?

Answer: BEFORE. Because clock timing is absolute and inflexible, clock nets are routed immediately after CTS to secure the fastest, straightest, top-layer metal tracks. Once the clock is frozen, the rest of the millions of data nets are routed around it.

6. Routing (Global, Track, Detailed)

Q65 What are the three stages of Routing?

Answer:
1. Global Routing: Assigns nets to general regions (Gcells) to plan the topology and analyze overall congestion. No physical metal is drawn.
2. Track Assignment: Assigns nets to specific horizontal/vertical tracks within the Gcells on specific metal layers.
3. Detailed Routing: Draws the actual geometric metal polygons and drops Vias to connect the pins, strictly obeying all DRC rules.

Q66 Why are adjacent metal layers routed in perpendicular directions (e.g., M2 Horizontal, M3 Vertical, M4 Horizontal)?

Answer: To minimize Cross-Layer Coupling Capacitance. If M2 and M3 were both horizontal and ran parallel on top of each other, they would form a massive parallel-plate capacitor, causing severe crosstalk and RC delay. Perpendicular crossing minimizes the overlap area to tiny squares.

Q67 What is "Layer Promotion"?

Answer: If a data path is failing Setup time due to high RC wire delay, the router will "promote" the net from lower, thin, high-resistance metal layers (e.g., M2/M3) to upper, thick, low-resistance metal layers (e.g., M7/M8). The signal travels faster, fixing the timing violation.

Q68 Tricky Can a router fix a Hold Time violation using layer demotion?

Answer: Yes, though less common than buffer insertion. The router can intentionally detour a wire or "demote" it to a highly resistive lower layer. This intentionally slows down the fast data signal so it safely meets the hold requirement.

Q69 How does the Detailed Router fix Antenna Rule Violations?

Answer: It breaks the long metal line near the gate and inserts a Layer Hop (Jumper) to a higher metal layer. If routing congestion prevents layer hopping, it inserts an Antenna Diode standard cell adjacent to the gate.

Q70 What is an L-Shape vs Z-Shape routing pattern?

Answer: When connecting two points, an L-shape uses one horizontal segment and one vertical segment (requires 1 Via). A Z-shape uses a horizontal, vertical, and another horizontal segment (requires 2 Vias). The router prefers L-shapes to minimize via usage, as vias add resistance and lower yield.

Q71 What are the negative impacts of excessive Vias?

Answer: Vias have high electrical resistance (hurting timing), are highly susceptible to Electromigration (EM) failure due to current crowding, and reduce manufacturing yield (a misaligned via causes an open circuit). Routers try to minimize via count aggressively.

Q72 How does the router fix a Crosstalk (Glitch) violation?

Answer:
1. Spacing: Physically separates the Aggressor and Victim wires.
2. Shielding: Drops a grounded $V_{SS}$ wire between them.
3. Upsizing: Swaps the Victim's driving gate to a higher drive strength cell so it can hold the wire voltage steady against the aggressor.

Q73 What is Pin Density / Pin Access Congestion?

Answer: Sometimes Gcell routing capacity is perfectly fine, but a highly complex multi-pin cell (like an Arithmetic Logic Unit) has too many input pins physically packed into a tiny area. The router cannot drop vias down to all the pins without violating minimum spacing DRCs. (Fixed by adding Cell Padding during placement).

Q74 Define "Search and Repair" in Detailed Routing.

Answer: After the initial detailed routing pass, there will be thousands of minor DRC violations (shorts, spacing errors). The router enters iterative "Search and Repair" loops, ripping up violating nets and re-routing them via alternate detours until zero DRCs remain.

Q75 What is DFM (Design For Manufacturability) redundant via insertion?

Answer: After routing is complete, the tool scans the design for any single, standalone vias. Wherever space permits, it drops a second, identical via right next to it (Double Via). If a dust particle ruins one via during fabrication, the redundant via saves the chip from a fatal open-circuit.

7. Physical Verification (DRC, LVS, ERC)

Q76 What does DRC stand for, and what is its purpose?

Answer: Design Rule Check. It verifies that the geometric polygons drawn by the EDA tool obey the physical manufacturing limits of the foundry's photolithography equipment.

Q77 Name three common DRC rules.

Answer:
1. Minimum Width: A metal wire cannot be thinner than $X\mu m$ (prevents breaking).
2. Minimum Spacing: Two metal wires must be at least $Y\mu m$ apart (prevents shorts).
3. Minimum Enclosure: A metal pad must overlap a via by at least $Z\mu m$ on all sides to ensure connection even if mask alignment shifts slightly.

Q78 What is an EOL (End of Line) Spacing Rule?

Answer: At the very tip of a wire, the optical photolithography light tends to bleed outward more than on the long edges. To prevent the tip of the wire from shorting with a perpendicular wire, the required spacing at the "End of Line" is significantly larger than standard parallel wire spacing.

Q79 What is LVS?

Answer: Layout Versus Schematic. It verifies that the physical geometry actually matches the intended logical design.

Q80 Explain the two-step process of how LVS actually works.

Answer:
1. Extraction: The LVS tool scans the layout polygons. Whenever it sees Poly crossing active diffusion, it identifies a transistor. It traces the metal layers to see what is connected, generating a brand new SPICE netlist purely from the shapes.
2. Comparison: It runs graph isomorphism algorithms to compare the extracted layout SPICE netlist against the golden synthesized RTL netlist. They must match perfectly.

Q81 What causes an LVS "Short"?

Answer: Two different logical nets (e.g., `data_A` and `data_B`) have physically touched in the layout, fusing into one single electrical net. This most commonly happens when standard cell pins are placed too closely, or power meshes inadvertently bridge.

Q82 What causes an LVS "Open"?

Answer: A logical net is broken in the layout. This happens when the router fails to connect a pin, or when a via is missing between two metal layers, leaving the circuit electrically disconnected.

Q83 What is ERC?

Answer: Electrical Rule Check. It scans the layout for catastrophic electrical errors that DRC/LVS might miss. For example: floating gate inputs, N-wells without $V_{DD}$ taps, P-substrates without GND taps, or connecting a PMOS source directly to GND.

Q84 What is Metal Density / Metal Fill?

Answer: Foundries require every region of the chip to have a specific density of metal (e.g., $30\%$ to $70\%$). If an area is too empty, the Chemical Mechanical Polishing (CMP) step will over-polish and create physical "dishing" craters. The tool automatically drops disconnected, floating polygons (Metal Fill) into empty spaces to satisfy density rules.

Q85 Why does inserting Metal Fill degrade timing?

Answer: Placing floating metal chunks near active signal wires increases their parasitic capacitance. Post-Fill STA must be run because this extra capacitance can push a critical path into a Setup violation at the very last minute.

8. Engineering Change Orders (ECO) & Signoff

Q86 What is an ECO?

Answer: Engineering Change Order. A manual or automated surgical fix applied to the design late in the flow to repair a functional bug or a minor timing violation without ripping up the entire floorplan and starting over.

Q87 Define a Pre-Mask ECO.

Answer: The design has finished routing, but the GDSII has not been sent to the foundry yet. You are free to add new standard cells, delete cells, and rewire. The tool just nudges surrounding cells slightly to make room. Very cheap to do.

Q88 Define a Post-Mask (Metal-Only) ECO.

Answer: The design is currently being manufactured in the fab. The base layers (Diffusion, Poly, Contacts) are permanently frozen in silicon. You cannot add or move any transistors. You can only change the upper Metal and Via mask layers, which have not been printed yet.

Q89 Tricky How do you fix a logic bug in a Metal-Only ECO if you can't add new transistors?

Answer: You use the Spare Cells that were sprinkled across the chip during the placement phase! You modify the metal layers to disconnect the bad logic, route the signals to the nearby spare gates (AND, OR, MUX), and route the corrected signal back to the main path.

Q90 What happens if the required spare cell is 2000$\mu m$ away from the bug?

Answer: You are in trouble. Routing a signal 2000$\mu m$ across the chip to reach the spare cell and back will incur massive RC delay, almost certainly causing a fatal Setup violation on that path. This is why spare cells must be densely and evenly distributed during floorplanning.

Q91 What are the inputs to the Formal Equivalence Checking (Logic Equivalence Check - LEC) tool?

Answer: The tool takes the Golden RTL (pre-synthesis) and compares it mathematically against the final Post-Route Gate-Level Netlist. It proves that synthesis, optimization, CTS, and routing did not alter the intended logical function of the chip.

Q92 If STA proves the timing is perfect, why do we run Gate-Level Simulation (GLS)?

Answer: STA is purely mathematical and ignores circuit initialization state (X-propagation), reset sequences, and asynchronous clock crossings. GLS actually runs test vectors through the final netlist to verify the chip boots up correctly and that reset/X-states don't lock the logic.

Q93 What is IR Drop Sign-off (e.g., RedHawk / Voltus)?

Answer: The final verification of the power grid. It ensures that under the worst-case dynamic switching scenario (e.g., maximum vector toggle activity), the voltage reaching every single standard cell never drops below the minimum threshold required for timing and functionality.

Q94 What is Tapeout?

Answer: The final milestone of the physical design cycle. All DRCs, LVS, ERC, and Timing checks are 100% clean. The final GDSII database is locked, signed off, and electronically transmitted to the foundry (TSMC/Intel/Samsung) for mask generation and fabrication.

Q95 What is a "Respin"?

Answer: If the chip fails after Tapeout (the manufactured silicon comes back and doesn't work), the engineering team must find the bug, fix the RTL/Layout, and pay the foundry for a completely new set of masks. This costs millions of dollars and delays the product by months.

Q96 Why are setup violations fixed by inserting buffers on the clock path instead of the data path?

Answer: Wait—you do NOT insert buffers on the clock path to fix setup! That is Useful Skew, which is dangerous. You fix setup by optimizing the data path (upsizing cells, LVT swapping). You ONLY insert buffers into the data path to fix Hold violations.

Q97 What is "Density Gradient" DRC?

Answer: It is not enough to just have 50% metal density. If one side of a 10$\mu m$ window has 90% metal and the other side has 10%, the CMP polisher will tilt and cause uneven thickness. Density must transition smoothly (low gradient) across the chip.

Q98 What does `report_qor` provide in standard PD tools?

Answer: Report Quality of Results. It provides a high-level summary of the layout's health: WNS, TNS, total cell area, standard cell utilization percentage, and routing congestion metrics.

Q99 Explain the "Sign-off Corner" philosophy.

Answer: You do not Tapeout based on "Typical" performance. The chip is signed off only when it passes STA across all extreme corners (SS, FF, RCworst, Cbest) across temperature extremes (-40C to 125C). If it passes the worst-case sign-off corners, the foundry guarantees the silicon will work.

Q100 The Final Test: If you have a Hold violation of -10ps, and a Setup slack of +5ps on the exact same path, what do you do?

Answer: You are in a "Timing Deadlock" (or Ping-Pong). You need to add a 10ps delay buffer to fix Hold, but doing so drops Setup to -5ps (Violation!).
The Fix: You must speed up the path first (e.g., swap a slow gate for an LVT cell) to increase your Setup slack to > +10ps. Then you can safely insert the hold buffer.
